# Capstone Reflection: Engineering Trust in AI-Native Systems
**CIT AI-Native Engineering Programme · Semester 1 · Assessment 5**  
**Word Count:** 418 words

Building the Extension Request Sorter provided profound insight into the engineering discipline required to deploy Large Language Models within mission-critical institutional operations. When developing software for academic environments, the central challenge is rarely whether an LLM can perform natural language parsing; rather, it is whether that intelligence can be safely bounded by deterministic rules, ethical guarantees, and rigorous human accountability.

Early in the project lifecycle, the temptation was to allow the LLM to perform both extraction and policy evaluation in a single unified prompt. However, rapid experimentation revealed why this approach is fundamentally flawed: LLMs exhibit subtle decision drift when reasoning across edge boundaries, occasionally rationalizing compassionate exceptions that violate syllabus regulations. The pivotal architectural breakthrough was establishing strict separation of concerns. By constraining the LLM exclusively to factual extraction (`course`, `days`, `reason`, `summary`) and delegating 100% of the policy logic (`recommend()`) to pure Python, policy enforcement became completely reproducible, auditable, and modifiable through configuration constants.

The project also confronted the critical necessity of defensive data sanitization and student privacy. Unstructured extension requests often contain highly sensitive personal information, ranging from acute medical crises to severe family bereavement. Trusting an AI model to redact its own summaries is insufficient. By implementing a deterministic Python privacy scrubber that searches for medical diagnoses and enforces neutral standardized phrasing, we guaranteed that health records remain strictly confidential and protected from institutional data leakage. Furthermore, verifying course codes against `courses.csv` and checking that requested days were explicitly written by the student—rather than hallucinated from phrases like "submit by Monday"—eliminated common AI failure modes before policy rules were applied.

Ultimately, this project reinforced the indispensable paradigm of Human-in-the-Loop engineering. An automated system should never masquerade as a judge or final arbiter of human circumstances; its ethical purpose is to eliminate administrative toil so that educators can concentrate their energy on empathy and individualized evaluation. The system’s three-way triage (`within_policy`, `needs_faculty_review`, `needs_more_info`) deliberately avoids binary approval or rejection. By routing non-routine circumstances to faculty review and preserving raw context exclusively for authorized educators, the tool respects institutional boundaries while preserving student dignity. Mastering this balance between generative capability and deterministic verification represents the true core of AI-native engineering.
